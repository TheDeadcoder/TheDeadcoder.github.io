When the cooling fails: teaching a 7B model to run a datacenter

Datacenter operators keep cooling and power alive by reading a dashboard and typing the right command. I built a physics simulator of that job and trained a 7B model in it, first by imitating a larger reasoning model and then with reinforcement learning against the live simulation. It went from waiting to acting, but it never resolved a hard incident, and a closer look at the evaluation changes what its numbers can claim.

A datacenter is a building full of heat. Every server turns the power it draws into heat, and a row of cooling units carries it away. When one of those units fails, someone in the operations center sees the alert, reads the dashboard and decides what to do: which unit to inspect, which survivors to push harder, which racks to shed. When the utility power drops, batteries carry the load while a diesel generator starts, and the clock is running.

I wanted to know whether a small language model could learn that job. Not talk about it, but read a live dashboard and type commands that a physical system obeys. Fluent text earns nothing here. A command that sounds right but names the wrong unit wastes a step, and the temperatures do not wait. The physics decides what worked.

That rules out the usual shortcuts. There is no dataset of expert operators working through these incidents, and nobody rehearses a cooling cascade on real hardware. So the project builds the whole chain: a physics simulator of a small facility, a dataset written by a larger reasoning model playing inside it, and a 7B model trained first to imitate that teacher and then to practise against the live simulation. This post walks through all three, the head-to-head results, and what a closer look at the evaluation says about them.

A datacenter in a text box

The simulator, DC-Ops, is built on OpenEnv1, an open framework for packaging the environments that agents learn in. It models a small facility: two zones with twenty racks and 160 kW of IT load, four cooling units known as CRACs, two UPS battery systems, a diesel generator and a transfer switch between utility and generator power. Temperatures follow a lumped thermal model: each zone stores heat, the servers add it, the cooling units remove it, and the walls exchange it with the outside. Batteries drain while they carry the load, and the generator needs time to start.

The agent gets no state vector. It reads the same text dashboard an operator would, with the alert, the state of every unit, temperatures and power readings, and answers with one of fourteen commands, such as diagnose CRAC-3 or set_fan_speed CRAC-2 100. Each command advances the simulation, and the next screen shows what happened.

Six scenarios cover cooling and power at three levels of difficulty, from units that overcool the room to a double cooling failure and a full loss of utility power. Each scenario injects its fault and decides for itself when the incident counts as resolved, and every step earns a reward of six parts: thermal safety, power safety, efficiency, progress, procedure and the quality of the action. The simulator is open source and runs live on Hugging Face Spaces, so you can try an incident in your browser.

Three steps of training

Without expert logs, the training data has to come from somewhere else. The pipeline has three phases, and each one hands its result to the next.

A teacher plays the scenarios

A reasoning model, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B from the DeepSeek-R1 family2, played live episodes in the simulator. At every turn it wrote a free chain of thought, a short structured reasoning block and one command. Of the 1,520 episodes it played, 1,083 survived filtering, which dropped episodes with no usable turn, an invalid command or an escalation to a human. The teacher was far from perfect: it resolved 179 of the 1,083, about one in six.

A 7B student imitates it

The student is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct3, fine-tuned through a small LoRA adapter. It is not a reasoning model, so the teacher’s chain of thought was cut out of every turn: the student learns to write the short reasoning block and the command, nothing else. This phase used the 934 episodes from the six scenarios, 7,107 turns in all.

The student practises against the simulator

Imitation copies the teacher, mistakes included. To go further, the student practised with GRPO4, reinforcement learning that compares several attempts at the same problem instead of training a separate critic. Each training prompt is a live screen, captured either at the start of an incident or a few scripted moves in. The model writes eight replies to that screen, each reply’s command runs in its own fresh copy of the simulator, and the replies that score above their group’s average become more likely.

The physics score has the widest range. It replays the screen’s history in a fresh simulator, runs the command, lets four more steps pass and rewards replies that leave the facility healthier, with a bonus if the incident resolves along the way. The command rules encode what an operator would expect, such as diagnosing the failed unit first and leaving broken units alone. Every phase, the teacher included, ran on a single AMD Instinct MI300X, and getting the training stack to work on AMD’s ROCm software took its own set of fixes, which the repository documents.

What training showed

Imitation did its job before RL began. At every logged step of RL, every reply was well formed: a reasoning block of reasonable length and one known command. The format score never moved from 0.15, and since GRPO learns only from differences within a group, all of the learning went to the other three scores.

The mean training reward rose 66% from the first 50 logged steps to the last 50, and most of that came early. By quarters of the run it averaged 1.33, 1.38, 1.41 and 1.18. The repeat penalty, which punished redoing a scripted warm-up command, had fallen to zero by the end.

Those numbers say the model got better at earning its reward. Whether it got better at running a datacenter is a question for the evaluation.

Head to head with the base model

To see what training changed, I ran the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and the trained model on the two hard scenarios, the cooling cascade (A4) and the power failure (B4), with five runs each and ten steps per run.

The change in behavior is the clearest result. The base model waited on 92 of its 100 steps. The trained model waited once. On the cooling cascade it opened every run by diagnosing CRAC-1, one of the two failed units, and then adjusted setpoints for the rest of the run, almost always on the two surviving units and mostly to 22°C. Its mean reward was 0.430 against the base model’s 0.041, and each of its five runs scored higher than every base run.

The power failure is murkier. The trained model sent start_generator in the first two steps of every run and then turned to rack loads. Nine of its 40 rack commands named racks that do not exist, such as A-4 for A-04, and the simulator rejected them. Of the 31 it accepted, 17 raised a rack above its starting 8 kW, the opposite of shedding load during an outage. Four of its five runs ended below zero, and its mean of −0.092 against the base model’s −0.147 hides a spread from −0.388 to +0.540.

No run resolved either incident, and the logs show why. Each scenario counts as solved only after the facility has held steady for two steps and a specific check has been made: the cooling cascade needs both failed units diagnosed, CRAC-1 and CRAC-3, and the power failure needs a UPS diagnosed. In twenty runs, neither model ever diagnosed CRAC-3 or a UPS.

Two more details temper the gains. In the power failure the generator starts on its own: the opening alert already says so, and in the simulator a run of plain waits brings it online exactly as fast as one that starts with start_generator. And the repeat penalty that reached zero in training did not stop loops: ten times, the trained model sent the very command it had just sent. In training it was only ever compared with a short scripted history, and at each step the model sees the current screen, not a list of what it has already done.

What the evaluation can and cannot claim

A review of the evaluation code turned up six problems. Five now have fixes written, and one is still open. None of the runs above has been repeated with the fixes, so every number in this post comes from the original runs, and the points below are insights into what those numbers can claim.

What the numbers support

The cooling-cascade gap was measured from one starting screen that the model had trained on, at half the scenario’s horizon, and scored with the reward it was trained to raise. It shows that training changed the policy. It does not yet show that the model handles a cooling failure better by measures it was never trained on.

What to take from it

  • Imitation teaches the format. From the first step of RL, every reply had the right shape. Here, 7,107 teacher turns were enough to give a 7B model a strict output format.
  • Training changed the behavior, but which phase did it is open. The trained model acted where the base model waited and aimed most of its commands at the right subsystem. Without an imitation-only baseline, the share that RL contributed is unknown.
  • Read the success check before the reward curve. Both hard scenarios needed a specific diagnosis before they could count as solved, and no run made it. A rising training curve could not show that. The logs could.
  • Check that seeds change what the agent sees. One ignored argument made five runs into one and left the training set with 48 distinct screens. A test that two seeds give two different screens would have caught it.

Limitations

This is a project, not a paper. The evidence covers one 7B model in one simulated facility, and the evaluation covers two scenarios with five runs per model, all starting from the same screen. The scores come from the simulator’s own reward, no run resolved a hard incident, and none of the fixes has been rerun yet.
