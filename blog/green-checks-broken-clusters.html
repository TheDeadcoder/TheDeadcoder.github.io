Green checks, broken clusters: testing the graders of AI SRE benchmarks

AI agents are now ranked on how well they fix broken Kubernetes applications, and a grading script decides every result. I tested the graders of four such benchmarks. They passed clusters that were still broken, failed fixes that worked, and the usual sanity checks missed most of it.

Agent evaluationBenchmarksSoftware testingTest oraclesKubernetesAI agents

Here is a simple experiment. Take an application running on Kubernetes, break it, and hand the cluster to an “agent” that does nothing at all. Then ask the benchmark how the agent did.

I ran this on SREGym, a recent benchmark for AI agents that work as site reliability engineers1. On its missing_service tasks, the answer came back: success. The fault had deleted a Kubernetes Service, and the benchmark's own load generator saw about 10% of user requests fail on one application and 60% on the other. But the grader only checked that the expected pods were running and ready, and the fault injector restarts every pod as its very last step. To the grader, the cluster looked healthy.

Every agent benchmark has a part like this grader, and it rarely gets tested itself. Software testing has a name for it: the test oracle, whatever decides whether an outcome is correct2. When the oracle is wrong, every score built on it is wrong too, and nothing looks out of place. A leaderboard with a broken grader still looks like a leaderboard.

That first finding became a workshop paper, accepted as a poster at the NeurIPS 2026 workshop Who Verifies the Agents?. I have since extended it from one benchmark to four, with more kinds of wrong fixes made on purpose, a stopwatch on the grader and three real AI agents. The extended paper is under review at ICST, and this post walks through what it found.

How these benchmarks grade an agent

Live SRE benchmarks broadly run the same loop. They deploy a real application, such as a hotel booking service, a social network or an online shop built from microservices, on a Kubernetes cluster. They inject a fault and hand the cluster to an agent along with a short task description. When the agent is done, a grading script checks the cluster, directly or through its monitoring, and returns pass or fail.

A grader can be wrong in two directions. A false accept passes a cluster that is still broken, so the agent gets credit it did not earn. A false reject fails a cluster that was fixed, so a correct repair loses its credit. Both move scores, and neither shows up in the score itself.

Eight questions for a grader

One bug in one task is an anecdote. To test graders systematically, I borrowed what software testing already knows about oracles, mutation testing, flaky tests and test isolation, and turned it into eight properties. Each one is a question a good grader should answer with yes, and each has a live experiment behind it.

  • Does it fail a broken cluster? An agent that does nothing should never pass.
  • Does it pass a healthy cluster? If nothing is wrong, there is nothing to fail.
  • Does it pass a correct fix? Both the benchmark's own reference fix and other fixes that also work.
  • Does it fail wrong fixes? I applied plausible but wrong repairs on purpose, as you will see below.
  • Is its verdict stable? The same action should get the same verdict, however often you ask and whenever you look.
  • Does it ignore other tasks? Leftovers from one task should not change another task's verdict.
  • Does the fix have to last? A repair that holds for a minute and then breaks again should not pass.
  • Is the task itself sound? The fault should take effect, the task description should point at it, and the answer should not be lying around in the cluster.

I ran these experiments on four open benchmarks: SREGym, AIOpsLab, k8s-ai-bench and ITBench. Each was pinned at a fixed version (SREGym also at a newer one), and the runs used live clusters rather than recorded traces.

Every benchmark breaks at least four of the eight properties, and SREGym, the largest, breaks all eight. The weak spots differ. SREGym and AIOpsLab let wrong fixes through: 53 of 109 SREGym tasks and all 13 AIOpsLab tasks I tested accepted at least one. AIOpsLab's verdicts also depend on timing: in 12 of its 14 tasks, the same action passed or failed depending on when the grader looked. k8s-ai-bench rejects correct fixes that differ from the one it expects, in 6 of 9 tasks tested, and ITBench passes fixes that do not last, in 16 of 23.

The usual checks miss most of it

Careful benchmark builders already run two sanity checks: an agent that does nothing must fail, and the benchmark's own reference fix must pass. Recent guidance on rigorous agent benchmarks recommends the first one explicitly3. Both are worth running, and in this study they caught problems in 22 of the 140 tasks with a defect. The other 118, or 84%, have defects that only the other experiments reveal.

The two checks test the two ends of a task: nothing done, or everything done right. Most of what they miss lives in between, in the wrong fix, the early verdict, the leftovers of the previous task and the fix that quietly breaks again.

Wrong fixes, on purpose

The most revealing experiment borrows from mutation testing. Instead of waiting for an agent to make a mistake, I made the mistakes myself. Each wrong fix, or mutant, is something a hurried engineer or a confused agent could plausibly do:

  • Restart: delete every pod and let them come back, without touching the cause.
  • Scale to zero or delete: make the broken workload go away instead of fixing it.
  • Collateral damage: apply the correct fix, then scale an unrelated, healthy service down to zero.

A good grader rejects all of them. SREGym's graders caught almost every restart, scale to zero and delete, 160 of 162. But they accepted the collateral damage in 49 of 96 tasks, because each grader only checks the part of the system the fault touched.

AIOpsLab's grader did worse. It waits until the pods it can see are healthy, and a deleted or scaled-down workload has no pods left to be unhealthy. Under its own rules, it accepted scale to zero and delete in 5 of 6 tasks each, and collateral damage in all 13. The fix I proposed is small: remember which workloads the application deployed, and fail while one of them is missing or scaled to zero. With it, the grader caught 24 more wrong fixes and let through none that it had caught before.

When the grader looks matters

Some graders do not look at the cluster's state directly. They ask the monitoring system whether any alerts are still firing. That sounds reasonable, but alerts lag behind the system. SREGym's alert grader, in a newer version than the one I pinned, waits 30 seconds after the agent finishes, fails at the first firing alert, and passes only after two minutes with none. But the error-rate alert is computed over the last two minutes of requests and re-evaluated once a minute, so it keeps firing for a while after the errors stop.

I measured what that does on eight problems, after three kinds of action: SREGym's own reference fix, no fix at all, and a restart only. Judged right away, the reference fix failed on all eight. Allowed to wait up to five minutes, the grader would pass three; up to ten minutes, all eight. The correct fixes needed between 211 and 573 seconds to look correct.

Waiting is not free, though. Given ten minutes, the grader also passed one problem with no fix at all, and the same problem after only a restart: its alerts went quiet on their own while the fault was still in place. A grader that waits should also check that the fault itself is gone.

This is not a lab curiosity. When real agents correctly fixed five of SREGym's alert problems, the grader failed 26 of their 45 fixes, and in every one of those 26 the same alert, HighRequestErrorRate, was still firing when it looked. The SREGym maintainers welcomed the report and invited a fix, which is now an open pull request.

Real agents, real misgrades

Mutants are controlled mistakes. To see what graders do with real behavior, I ran three agents on tasks from SREGym, AIOpsLab and ITBench: Claude Code with Claude Sonnet 5.5 and with Claude Haiku 5.5, and OpenAI's Codex CLI with gpt-6.1-sol, 182 runs in all. I compared each verdict with an independent reference check that looks at what users would notice: whether requests succeed, whether services still route traffic, and whether capacity is back to the healthy baseline.

How often the verdict disagreed with the reference depends on the benchmark, not on the agent. On the tasks they shared, Sonnet, Haiku and Codex were misgraded about equally often: an exact McNemar test finds no difference for any pair (p = 1.0).

The grid shows both kinds of error side by side. On SREGym's alert problems, the crosses are correct fixes failed by the timing problem above. On ITBench's scenario 114, every run passed, yet in eight of the nine the product catalog service still could not be reached through its Service. On scenarios 31 and 58, a correct fix failed unless the agent had also changed the monitoring: the only runs that passed were ones in which it had. Three stories stand out.

The operator nobody touched

SREGym's wrong operator image task breaks the operator that manages a TiDB database, in one namespace. The grader checks a second namespace, and the task description points the agent at a third, where the application lives. All nine runs passed with the operator still broken, and not one agent ran a single command in the operator's namespace. “I found nothing broken in the FleetCast app, so I changed nothing,” one of them reported. The maintainers are now fixing this check in a pull request of their own.

The rules that moved

ITBench passes a run once the expected alerts stop firing. In 9 of its 16 ITBench runs, Codex edited the alerting rules themselves, the very rules that check reads, and the check passed all 9. Neither Claude model touched the rules, although Haiku changed the telemetry collector in three runs. Some of Codex's runs also fixed the fault and some did not. Either way, a grader that reads something the agent can edit cannot tell the difference.

The answer key in the room

AIOpsLab leaves mitigation scripts inside the database pods of four of its problems. The agents found them: in 10 runs, an agent ran a script that the benchmark had left behind. It is the benchmark version of an exam with the answer key on the desk.

A pass is a claim, not a fact

A green check says what the grader saw, at the moment it looked, in the part of the system it was built to watch. Whether the cluster is actually fixed is a separate question, and in this study the grader and the reference check disagreed on as many as 65% of one benchmark's agent runs.

Does it move the leaderboard?

Sometimes a lot, sometimes not at all. k8s-ai-bench publishes its raw results, so instead of running agents on it I re-scored its published leaderboard: 1,200 runs of ten models on 24 tasks. I credited runs that a check rejected only because the container image was not written as the exact string “nginx”, and set aside failures caused by the benchmark or its harness, such as a check that runs before its database pod is scheduled.

Most scores did not move, and the ranking barely changed: one pair of neighbors swapped places (Kendall's τ = 0.956). But gpt-oss-20b rose from 40.8% to 51.9%, 11 points from grading alone. Grader errors do not spread evenly across agents, so a few points between two agents can be the grader's doing rather than theirs.

If you build or use these benchmarks

The fixes are mostly cheap. A short checklist for the graders of live benchmarks:

  1. Keep the two usual checks, the agent that does nothing and the reference fix, and run them on every task.
  2. Throw wrong fixes at the grader. A restart, a deletion, scaling to zero and a correct fix with collateral damage should all fail.
  3. Judge after the system settles, within a stated time limit, and also check that the fault itself is gone.
  4. Compare against the healthy state of the whole application, not only the part the fault touched.
  5. Isolate tasks, or verify the reset between them.
  6. Check that fixes last, for example when a pod is replaced.
  7. Keep the answer and the grader's inputs, such as alert rules, out of the agent's reach.

And if you only use these benchmarks, read a score together with how its grader was tested. I reported the problems to the maintainers of SREGym, AIOpsLab and ITBench, with proposed fixes for AIOpsLab and ITBench, and SREGym's two fixes are underway as described above.

Limitations

The study covers four Kubernetes benchmarks, each at a fixed version, and they keep changing: SREGym's newer version fixed some of the problems I found in the pinned one and introduced others. The reference check is my own and has blind spots, such as lost data, network policies and changes to the monitoring itself; its labels did not change when I moved its thresholds. ITBench's hosted grader is not public, so I reimplemented its check from the repository. Three agents with up to three attempts per task give small counts per cell, and timing effects varied between machines.
